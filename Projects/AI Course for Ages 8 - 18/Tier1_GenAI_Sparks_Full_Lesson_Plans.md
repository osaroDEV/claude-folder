Codeillustrated Academy — GenAI Sparks (Ages 8–10)
====================================================

Full Lesson Plans — All 20 Sessions (Weeks 1–10)

Companion document to: Generative_AI_Course_Curriculum_Codeillustrated_Academy.md
Format: 2 × 60-minute live sessions/week — Session A (Concept), Session B (Studio)
All tool use is instructor-driven/projected or on shared, instructor-managed accounts — no individual student accounts.

## Week 1 — What Is Generative AI?

### Session A (Concept): "AI That Makes vs. AI That Sorts"

**Objective:** Students will distinguish between AI that recognizes/sorts things and AI that creates new things, and name one example of each.

**Materials:** Projector, ★Handout 1.1 "AI or Not AI?" picture cards (printed set).

- Warm-up (10 min): Ask "Has anyone ever asked a smart speaker or app for something and gotten a picture, story, or song back that didn't exist before?" Collect a few examples.
- Direct instruction (20 min): Show 4–5 familiar examples (a photo app that sorts pictures by face vs. an app that draws a new picture from words). Introduce the idea: "Some computer programs look at things and sort them. Some computer programs make brand-new things. We call the second kind 'generative' — like the word 'generate,' which means 'to make.'"
- Guided activity (20 min): As a class, sort ★Handout 1.1 cards into two piles: "Sorts/Recognizes" and "Makes Something New." Discuss each card together.
- Wrap-up (10 min): Each student names one generative AI example they've seen (or heard about) at home.

**Differentiation:** Pre-readers get picture-only cards; advanced students get an extra "trick card" that could belong in either pile, prompting discussion.

**Checks for understanding:** Cold-call 3–4 students to explain, in one sentence, the difference between "sorts" and "makes new."

### Session B (Studio): "Made by a Person or by AI?"

**Objective:** Students will practice identifying whether an example (picture, sentence, or object) was likely made by a person or by generative AI, using simple clues.

**Materials:** ★Handout 1.2 "Made By Who?" sorting mat and card set, timer.

- Warm-up (5 min): Recap Session A's two piles.
- Hands-on activity (35 min): In pairs, students sort ★Handout 1.2 cards onto a two-column mat ("Person-Made" / "AI-Made"), then discuss with a neighboring pair why they chose each answer. Instructor reveals answers as a class and discusses any surprises.
- Reflection (15 min): Class discussion — "What clues helped you decide?" Build a shared "clue list" on the board (e.g., "looks a little too perfect," "colors look strange," "doesn't quite make sense").
- Wrap-up (5 min): Preview Week 2 — "Next time, we'll see how AI actually learns to draw."

**Differentiation:** Provide a 2-clue vs. 4-clue version of the sorting mat depending on group.

**Checks for understanding:** Each pair reports one clue they used to the class.

## Week 2 — How AI Draws

### Session A (Concept): "Sketches, Patterns, and Guesses"

**Objective:** Students will explain that an AI drawing tool guesses what a sketch represents by comparing it to patterns it has seen before.

**Materials:** Projector, AutoDraw (instructor-driven), ★Handout 2.1 "Guess My Sketch" recording sheet.

- Warm-up (10 min): Instructor draws a quick, rough shape on the board and asks students to guess what it is. Discuss how they used "patterns they know" to guess.
- Direct instruction (20 min): Instructor opens AutoDraw and sketches 3–4 simple objects live, showing how the tool suggests polished icons based on the rough sketch. Explain: "The computer has seen thousands of drawings before, so it recognizes patterns in your sketch and guesses what you meant."
- Guided activity (20 min): Class calls out simple objects to sketch; instructor draws them in AutoDraw and the class predicts what suggestion will appear before revealing it.
- Wrap-up (10 min): Students fill in ★Handout 2.1 with one sketch idea they want to try next session.

**Differentiation:** Provide a picture-word bank for students who need help naming objects.

**Checks for understanding:** Ask 3 students to explain in their own words why the tool "guesses" instead of "just knowing."

### Session B (Studio): "AutoDraw Sketch Parade"

**Objective:** Students will each contribute a sketch to a shared AutoDraw session and compare how accurately the tool matched their intent.

**Materials:** AutoDraw (instructor-driven, shared screen or rotating small-group turns on managed devices), ★Handout 2.1 (completed from Session A).

- Warm-up (5 min): Students share their planned sketch idea from ★Handout 2.1.
- Hands-on activity (40 min): Rotate through small groups; each group directs the instructor (or uses a managed device under supervision) to sketch their idea in AutoDraw, compares the suggested icon to their original intent, and rates how close the match was (1–3 stars).
- Reflection (10 min): Gallery review of all sketches/results as a class; discuss which sketches were "easy" for the tool vs. "tricky."
- Wrap-up (5 min): Preview Week 3 — "Next week, AI will help us tell a whole story, not just draw one picture."

**Differentiation:** Extension: ask advanced students to sketch something unusual and predict whether the tool will recognize it before testing.

**Checks for understanding:** Each group states their star rating and one reason for it.

## Week 3 — AI That Tells Stories

### Session A (Concept): "Watching AI Write"

**Objective:** Students will describe, at a basic level, that a story-writing AI builds sentences based on patterns in lots of stories it has "read" before.

**Materials:** Projector, school-managed K–8 AI chat tool (instructor account only), ★Handout 3.1 "Story Idea" cards.

- Warm-up (10 min): Ask students to imagine writing a story about "a dragon who is afraid of the dark" — what might happen first, next, and last?
- Direct instruction (20 min): Instructor types a simple story prompt into the school-managed AI chat tool (projected), reads the result aloud, and discusses: "The AI put these words together because it has seen many, many stories before — it's guessing what would come next, sentence by sentence."
- Guided activity (20 min): As a class, pick one ★Handout 3.1 story-idea card and watch the instructor generate a story from it; pause partway through and have students predict what happens next before revealing the AI's actual continuation.
- Wrap-up (10 min): Discuss: "Was the AI's guess close to what you predicted? What was different?"

**Differentiation:** For younger/less confident readers, instructor reads the AI's story aloud slowly with picture support.

**Checks for understanding:** Ask students to give a thumbs-up/thumbs-down on whether the AI's story matched their prediction, then explain why.

### Session B (Studio): "Build-a-Story"

**Objective:** Students will collaboratively shape an AI-assisted story by contributing ideas and editing the result as a class.

**Materials:** School-managed AI chat tool (instructor-driven), whiteboard, ★Handout 3.1 story-idea cards.

- Warm-up (5 min): Vote as a class on a story-idea card to use.
- Hands-on activity (35 min): Instructor generates the story in short chunks (2–3 sentences at a time), pausing after each chunk so the class can vote on how the story should continue, feeding that idea back into the next prompt. Class edits any part they don't like by suggesting a change and re-generating that section.
- Reflection (15 min): Read the finished story aloud as a class; discuss which parts were "the AI's idea" vs. "our idea."
- Wrap-up (5 min): Preview Week 4 — "Next week we learn the exact words that help AI understand us better — we call them 'magic words.'"

**Differentiation:** Offer sentence-starter cards for students contributing ideas verbally.

**Checks for understanding:** Ask 2–3 students to point to one line in the final story and say whether it was "class idea" or "AI idea."

## Week 4 — Magic Words (Intro to Prompts)

*(Full detailed plan already developed in the curriculum document — reproduced here for completeness.)*

### Session A (Concept): "Magic Words"

**Objective:** Students will observe that changing the words in a prompt changes the AI's output, and describe one way to make a prompt "more specific."

**Materials:** Projector, instructor-managed guided image tool account, ★Handout 4.1 "Prompt Cards" (color, size, feeling, place words).

- Warm-up (10 min): "If you asked a friend to draw 'an animal,' what might they draw? What if you said 'a fluffy orange cat sitting in the sun'?"
- Direct instruction (20 min): Instructor runs the same short prompt twice on the guided tool ("a dog"), then a detailed version ("a small fluffy brown dog playing with a red ball in a park"), comparing results live. Introduce the word "prompt."
- Guided group activity (25 min): Class takes turns suggesting one added detail to a prompt; instructor runs it after each addition so students see the image evolve.
- Wrap-up (5 min): "What's one word you could add to a prompt to make it better?"

**Differentiation:** Picture-based prompt-card option for pre-readers; extension groups try a 4th descriptive detail and predict the result first.

**Checks for understanding:** Each group states one detail they added and what changed.

### Session B (Studio): "Prompt Play"

**Objective:** Students will practice building a prompt with 2–3 descriptive details and compare before/after results.

**Materials:** Instructor-managed guided image tool, ★Handout 4.1 prompt cards.

- Warm-up (5 min): Recap "magic words."
- Hands-on activity (40 min): Small groups (instructor drives the account, students dictate) write a simple prompt on a card, add 2–3 details, and run it; compare "before" and "after" images.
- Reflection (10 min): Gallery walk of results — "Which detail made the biggest difference?"
- Wrap-up (5 min): Preview Week 5 — "Next time we'll see what happens when AI gets it wrong."

**Differentiation:** Picture-based cards for pre-readers; extension groups add a 4th detail and predict first.

**Checks for understanding:** Each group states one detail added and its effect.

## Week 5 — When AI Gets It Wrong

### Session A (Concept): "Silly AI Mistakes"

**Objective:** Students will recognize that generative AI can make mistakes or "make things up," and give one example.

**Materials:** Projector, ★Handout 5.1 "Spot the Silly Mistake" example sheet (pre-selected AI outputs with errors, e.g., an image with extra fingers, a story with an inconsistency).

- Warm-up (10 min): "Has a grown-up or a computer ever told you something that turned out to be wrong? What did you do?"
- Direct instruction (20 min): Show 3–4 pre-selected examples of AI mistakes (funny image errors, a story detail that doesn't add up). Explain simply: "AI guesses based on patterns, so sometimes its guess is wrong or a little bit silly — that's called a 'mistake' or when it 'makes something up.'"
- Guided activity (20 min): As a class, go through ★Handout 5.1 and circle what's wrong in each example.
- Wrap-up (10 min): Discuss: "If AI can make mistakes, what should we always do before believing something it made?" (Answer: check with a grown-up or a trusted source.)

**Differentiation:** Provide a simplified 2-example version for younger/less-attentive groups; extension students find a 5th "mistake" independently if time allows.

**Checks for understanding:** Each student names one mistake they spotted.

### Session B (Studio): "Fix the AI's Mistake"

**Objective:** Students will identify an error in an AI-generated example and suggest a correction.

**Materials:** ★Handout 5.2 "Fix It!" worksheet (a set of AI-made images/short stories with an embedded error each), crayons/pencils.

- Warm-up (5 min): Recap Week 5 Session A's examples.
- Hands-on activity (40 min): In small groups, students work through ★Handout 5.2, circling the mistake in each example and drawing or writing what the "fixed" version should look like.
- Reflection (10 min): Groups share one fix with the class.
- Wrap-up (5 min): Preview Week 6 — "Next week: can you tell if a picture is real or made by AI?"

**Differentiation:** Provide fewer examples with more space for younger students; extension students write a full corrected sentence instead of just circling.

**Checks for understanding:** Each group explains one fix and why it corrects the mistake.

## Week 6 — Is It Real or AI-Made?

### Session A (Concept): "First Clues for Spotting AI"

**Objective:** Students will name at least two clues that might suggest an image or story was AI-made.

**Materials:** Projector, ★Handout 6.1 "Real or AI-Made?" image set (age-appropriate, clearly sourced examples).

- Warm-up (10 min): Show one clearly AI-made image and one clearly real photo; ask "which do you think is which, and why?"
- Direct instruction (20 min): Build a simple "clue list" with the class: things AI sometimes gets wrong (hands/fingers, text in images, reflections, background details that don't quite make sense). Emphasize AI is getting better at this, so it's not always easy — that's okay.
- Guided activity (20 min): Go through ★Handout 6.1 as a class, applying the clue list to each example.
- Wrap-up (10 min): Discuss: "Why does it matter if we can tell the difference?"

**Differentiation:** Provide fewer, more obvious examples for younger groups; extension examples are more subtle.

**Checks for understanding:** Each student names one clue from the list in their own words.

### Session B (Studio): "Real vs. AI-Made Sorting Game"

**Objective:** Students will apply clue-spotting skills independently to sort a new set of examples.

**Materials:** ★Handout 6.2 "Sorting Game" card set and mat, timer.

- Warm-up (5 min): Recap the clue list from Session A.
- Hands-on activity (35 min): In pairs, students sort ★Handout 6.2 cards into "Real" and "AI-Made" piles, then check answers as a class, discussing any tricky ones.
- Reflection (15 min): Which examples were hardest to tell apart, and why?
- Wrap-up (5 min): Preview Week 7 — "Next week: how to be safe and kind when using AI."

**Differentiation:** Provide a 2-clue checklist for scaffolding; extension pairs try to explain why a "trick" example was hard.

**Checks for understanding:** Each pair reports their trickiest card and their reasoning.

## Week 7 — Being Safe & Kind with AI

### Session A (Concept): "Rules for Using AI Safely"

**Objective:** Students will state at least two safety rules for using AI tools (e.g., never share personal information, always ask a trusted adult first).

**Materials:** Projector, ★Handout 7.1 "Safety Rules" poster/handout.

- Warm-up (10 min): "What are some things you already know to keep safe online?" (e.g., not sharing your address).
- Direct instruction (20 min): Introduce AI-specific safety rules: never type your name, address, school, or photos of yourself into any AI tool; always ask a trusted adult before using a new AI tool; remember AI is a tool, not a friend or an authority — it doesn't know you and can't take care of you.
- Guided activity (20 min): Read through ★Handout 7.1 together, discussing why each rule matters with a simple example for each.
- Wrap-up (10 min): Students each pick their "most important rule to remember" and share it.

**Differentiation:** Visual icon-based version of the handout for pre-readers.

**Checks for understanding:** Each student states one rule and a reason for it.

### Session B (Studio): "What Would You Do?"

**Objective:** Students will apply safety rules to realistic scenarios through role-play.

**Materials:** ★Handout 7.2 "What Would You Do?" scenario cards.

- Warm-up (5 min): Recap Week 7 Session A's rules.
- Hands-on activity (35 min): In small groups, students act out or discuss 3–4 ★Handout 7.2 scenarios (e.g., "An AI chat asks you what school you go to — what do you do?"), then present their response to the class.
- Reflection (15 min): Class discussion comparing responses; instructor reinforces correct safety choices.
- Wrap-up (5 min): Preview Week 8 — "Next week: AI helpers we might have at home."

**Differentiation:** Provide simplified scenario cards with picture support; extension groups write their own additional scenario.

**Checks for understanding:** Each group states the safety rule that applied to their scenario.

## Week 8 — AI Helpers at Home

### Session A (Concept): "Voice Assistants and Generated Answers"

**Objective:** Students will explain that voice assistants sometimes generate answers on the spot rather than reading from a fixed list.

**Materials:** Projector, recorded/simulated voice assistant examples (pre-approved, instructor-controlled — no live open internet queries).

- Warm-up (10 min): "Does anyone have a smart speaker or voice assistant at home? What have you asked it?"
- Direct instruction (20 min): Play 2–3 pre-approved recorded examples of a voice assistant answering a question, discussing how some answers are simple lookups (time, weather) while others are more "generated" (telling a joke, making up a short rhyme).
- Guided activity (20 min): As a class, sort a list of sample questions into "probably a simple lookup" vs. "probably generated on the spot."
- Wrap-up (10 min): Discuss: "Why is it good to know the difference?"

**Differentiation:** Provide picture/audio cues for pre-readers; extension students propose their own example question and guess which category it falls in.

**Checks for understanding:** Each student sorts one new example question correctly with a reason.

### Session B (Studio): "Interview a Smart Speaker"

**Objective:** Students will practice asking varied questions and categorizing the type of response received.

**Materials:** Instructor-managed/simulated voice assistant demo, ★Handout 8.1 "Interview Sheet."

- Warm-up (5 min): Recap lookup vs. generated answers.
- Hands-on activity (35 min): In small groups, students take turns suggesting a question for the instructor to ask the (simulated/controlled) assistant, then record the answer type on ★Handout 8.1.
- Reflection (15 min): Share interesting answers with the class; discuss which were surprising.
- Wrap-up (5 min): Preview Week 9 — "Next week, you start building your very own AI-assisted project!"

**Differentiation:** Provide a shorter list of pre-approved sample questions for groups who need it.

**Checks for understanding:** Each group reports one question and its answer-type classification.

## Week 9 — My First AI-Assisted Creation

### Session A (Concept): "Project Kickoff"

**Objective:** Students will plan a simple project combining a drawing and a short AI-assisted story.

**Materials:** ★Handout 9.1 "My Project Plan" worksheet, crayons/pencils.

- Warm-up (10 min): Recap favorite activities/tools from the tier so far (sketching, story-building, prompts).
- Direct instruction (15 min): Explain the final project: each student will make a simple drawing (using AutoDraw, instructor-supported) and a short AI-assisted story to go with it, to share with family at the Week 10 showcase.
- Guided planning (25 min): Students complete ★Handout 9.1, sketching their idea and writing 2–3 words/details they want in their story.
- Wrap-up (10 min): Share plans with a partner for feedback.

**Differentiation:** Provide sentence starters and picture word banks for students who need support planning.

**Checks for understanding:** Each student can state their project idea in one sentence.

### Session B (Studio): "Build Time"

**Objective:** Students will produce a first draft of their AI-assisted drawing and story with instructor support.

**Materials:** AutoDraw, school-managed AI chat tool (instructor-driven), ★Handout 9.1 (completed plans).

- Warm-up (5 min): Review individual project plans.
- Hands-on activity (40 min): Rotating small-group instructor support: each student directs their AutoDraw sketch and a short AI-assisted story addition based on their plan; instructor helps refine and assembles a simple printed or on-screen "project card" per student.
- Reflection (10 min): Students briefly show a partner their in-progress project.
- Wrap-up (5 min): Preview Week 10 — "Next week is our showcase — you'll share your project with your family!"

**Differentiation:** Extra instructor 1:1 time for students needing more support; extension students add an additional detail or a second story part.

**Checks for understanding:** Each student can point to one part of their project and explain their choice.

## Week 10 — Showcase

### Session A (Concept): "Showcase Prep"

**Objective:** Students will practice describing their project clearly and confidently.

**Materials:** Completed student projects, ★Handout 10.1 "What I Made" presentation card.

- Warm-up (10 min): Recap what "showcase" means — sharing your work with family and friends.
- Direct instruction (15 min): Model a simple presentation structure: "This is my picture of ___. My story is about ___. My favorite part was ___."
- Guided practice (25 min): Students fill out ★Handout 10.1 and practice their mini-presentation with a partner, giving each other one piece of friendly feedback.
- Wrap-up (10 min): Whole-group practice run with 2–3 volunteers presenting to the class.

**Differentiation:** Provide sentence-starter cards; pair less-confident presenters with a supportive partner.

**Checks for understanding:** Each student can deliver their 3-sentence presentation to a partner without prompting.

### Session B (Studio): "Showcase & Celebration"

**Objective:** Students will present their AI-assisted project to family/guests and reflect on what they learned across the tier.

**Materials:** Student projects, ★Handout 10.1 cards, "GenAI Spark" certificates (printed).

- Warm-up (5 min): Final reminders on presentation structure.
- Showcase (40 min): Students present their projects to family/guests in small rotating groups or one at a time, depending on class size; instructor facilitates and keeps time.
- Reflection & celebration (10 min): Whole-group reflection — "What was your favorite thing you learned this tier?" Distribute "GenAI Spark" certificates.
- Wrap-up (5 min): Congratulate students; briefly preview GenAI Creators (ages 11–14) for those continuing.

**Differentiation:** Offer a "gallery walk" format (projects displayed, family circulates) as an alternative for very shy presenters.

**Checks for understanding:** Instructor observation checklist — each student successfully shared at least one sentence about their project.
