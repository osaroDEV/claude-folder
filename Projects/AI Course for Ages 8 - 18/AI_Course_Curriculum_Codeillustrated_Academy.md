## 1. Course Philosophy & Framework

Ages 8–18 span radically different cognitive and emotional stages, so this is not one course — it is three developmentally-tuned tiers that share a common backbone: the five big ideas that national AI-education researchers (the AI4K12 initiative, backed by CSTA and AAAI) recommend as the core of any K–12 AI literacy program:

1. **Perception** — computers perceive the world through sensors and data.
2. **Representation & Reasoning** — computers build internal models of the world and use rules or logic to act on them.
3. **Learning** — computers can improve at a task by learning from data and examples.
4. **Natural Interaction** — building AI that communicates naturally with people requires language, vision, and social knowledge.
5. **Societal Impact** — AI changes society, for better and worse, and carries real responsibility.

Each tier revisits all five ideas, but at increasing depth — a spiral curriculum rather than three disconnected courses. This mirrors the approach used by AI4K12's own grade-band charts (K–2, 3–5, 6–8, 9–12) and by Code.org's AI Discoveries (middle school) and AI Foundations (high school) courses, which likewise integrate ethics and responsible use into every unit rather than treating it as a bolt-on module.

Design principles for Codeillustrated Academy specifically:

- **No coding required to start.** Tier 1 is entirely block-based/unplugged. Text-based Python doesn't appear until Tier 2, and only lightly.
- **Build before you theorize.** Every session leads with a hands-on activity; concepts are introduced to explain what students just did, not before.
- **Ethics is not a unit, it's a thread.** Every session closes with a 5–10 minute "Think About It" discussion connected to that day's content.
- **Live cohort format.** Weekly synchronous sessions with an instructor, small group sizes, and a visible capstone/showcase at the end of each tier so parents and learners see tangible progress.

## 2. Program Structure at a Glance

| Tier | Name | Ages | Duration | Session Length | Format | Max Class Size |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | AI Explorers | 8–10 | 8 weeks | 60 min/week | Block-based + unplugged, live instructor | 10–12 |
| 2 | AI Builders | 11–14 | 10 weeks | 75 min/week | Block-based → light Python, live instructor | 14–16 |
| 3 | AI Innovators | 15–18 | 12 weeks | 90 min/week | Python/Jupyter, live instructor | 16–20 |

Recommended progression: Explorers → Builders → Innovators, though each tier is designed to also stand alone for a learner entering at that age with no prior AI exposure. A learner who completes all three will have moved from "AI is all around me" to a portfolio-ready capstone project suitable for college applications or competitions.

## 3. Tier 1 — AI Explorers (Ages 8–10)

### Overview

Big-idea focus (AI4K12 grade bands K–2/3–5): noticing AI in daily life, understanding that computers "see" and "learn" differently than people, and building healthy, kind habits around AI tools. No reading-heavy material; everything is activity-first, game-based, and discussion-driven.

**Tools:** Google's Quick, Draw!; Teachable Machine (Google); Scratch with AI/ML extensions (e.g., Cognimates or ml4kids blocks); printed "unplugged" card-sorting activities; simple voice assistant demos.

**Learning outcomes by end of tier:**
- Can give 3+ real-world examples of AI they encounter (recommendations, voice assistants, photo tagging).
- Understands that AI "learns" from examples, and that bad or unfair examples produce bad or unfair results.
- Can explain, in their own words, that AI is a tool, not a person, and knows to ask a trusted adult before sharing personal information with any AI tool.

### Weekly Session Breakdown

| Wk | Topic & Objective | Key Activity | Materials |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | What is AI? Spot it in daily life | "AI or Not AI?" sorting game with everyday objects/apps | Picture cards, sorting mat |
| 2 | How computers "see" (Perception) | Play Quick, Draw!; discuss how the computer guesses drawings | Laptop/tablet, projector |
| 3 | Teaching a machine (Learning by example) | Train a Teachable Machine model to recognize hand gestures | Laptop/tablet w/ webcam |
| 4 | How computers "think" (simple rules) | Unplugged flowchart: build a paper "robot" that follows if-then rules | Paper flowchart templates |
| 5 | Talking to computers (Natural Interaction) | Explore a voice assistant demo; build a simple rule-based chatbot in Scratch | Laptop/tablet, Scratch |
| 6 | Is AI always right? (Bias intro) | "Bias in training data" card game — train a pretend model on unfair examples | Card sets, worksheet |
| 7 | Being kind online & to AI (Ethics) | Discussion + role-play: privacy, honesty, asking a trusted adult | Scenario cards |
| 8 | Showcase | Mini "AI Show & Tell" — present a trained model or chatbot to family | Certificates, presentation time |

### Sample Full Lesson Plan — Week 3: "Teaching a Machine"

**Objective:** Students will train an image-recognition model using their own examples and explain, in simple terms, why the model needs many varied examples to work well.

**Materials:** Laptops/tablets with webcams, Teachable Machine website (teachablemachine.withgoogle.com), 3–4 everyday objects per student pair.

**Warm-up (10 min):** Quick recap game — "Where did we see AI last week?" Show of hands / popcorn share. Ask: "How do you think a computer 'learns' to recognize a drawing?"

**Direct instruction (15 min):** Instructor demonstrates Teachable Machine live: creates two classes ("thumbs up" / "thumbs down"), records ~20 webcam samples of each, trains the model live, tests it. Emphasize: the computer is not thinking like a person — it is finding patterns in the images it was shown.

**Hands-on activity (25 min):** In pairs, students create their own two-class model (e.g., "cup" vs. "book," or two hand gestures). They train it, test it with a partner, then intentionally "break" it by testing an object it wasn't trained on — observing and discussing the wrong guess.

**Wrap-up & reflection (10 min):** Discussion: "What happened when you showed it something new? Why do you think it guessed wrong?" Bridge to next week's bias lesson: "If we only showed it pictures of one thing, what would happen?"

**Differentiation:** Pair a less-confident reader with a stronger one; instructor pre-labels example objects for students who need it; extension for early finishers — try a 3rd class and see if accuracy changes.

**Checks for understanding:** Instructor circulates during hands-on time, asks each pair to explain in one sentence "why" their model made a mistake.

### Assessment Approach

No formal grades. Instructor uses a simple observation checklist (participation, one understanding-check question answered per session) and awards a sticker/digital badge per completed activity. Tier ends with an "AI Explorer" certificate presented at the Week 8 showcase.

## 4. Tier 2 — AI Builders (Ages 11–14)

### Overview

Big-idea focus (AI4K12 grade band 6–8): deeper treatment of representation & reasoning, an introduction to supervised learning as a category, natural-language interaction (chatbots), and case-study-driven societal impact (bias, misinformation, jobs). Light Python is introduced late in the tier as a bridge to Tier 3 — not a prerequisite.

**Tools:** Machine Learning for Kids (IBM Watson-based, free); MIT App Inventor or Scratch AI extensions; TensorFlow Playground (visual neural network explorer, no code); Google Colab (guided, copy-and-run Python) introduced in Week 8.

**Learning outcomes by end of tier:**
- Can describe what training data is and why labeling/quality matters.
- Can build and test a working classifier (image or text) using a no-code/low-code tool.
- Can explain at a conceptual level what a neural network does with inputs and weights.
- Can identify a real-world example of algorithmic bias and propose a fix.
- Has run and modified a short Python script that calls a trained model.

### Weekly Session Breakdown

| Wk | Topic & Objective | Key Activity | Materials |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | AI all around us — history & careers | Timeline-building activity; "AI careers" speed-round | Timeline cards |
| 2 | Data is the fuel | Data collection & labeling activity — students label a small shared dataset | Worksheet, sample images/text |
| 3 | Supervised learning in action | Build a text or image classifier in Machine Learning for Kids | Laptops, ML4Kids accounts |
| 4 | Neural networks, visually | Explore TensorFlow Playground; adjust layers/weights and observe | Laptops |
| 5 | Natural language & chatbots | Build a hybrid rule-based + ML chatbot | Laptops, ML4Kids/Scratch |
| 6 | Computer vision mini-project | Train + deploy an image classifier app (App Inventor) | Laptops, App Inventor |
| 7 | Bias, fairness & data ethics | Case studies (facial recognition, hiring algorithms) + fix-it activity | Case study handouts |
| 8 | Intro to Python for AI | First scripts in Colab; call a pre-trained model via simple API | Laptops, Colab |
| 9 | Capstone work session | Teams/individuals build final project with instructor support | Laptops, project tools |
| 10 | Capstone showcase & peer review | Present projects; structured peer feedback; certificates | Presentation setup |

### Capstone Options (choose one)

- A recycling/waste sorter using an image classifier.
- A mood-journal chatbot that responds to simple text input.
- A trivia or homework-helper chatbot.
- A custom "spot the difference" image classifier for a hobby (sports, animals, art styles).

### Sample Full Lesson Plan — Week 7: "Bias, Fairness & Data Ethics"

**Objective:** Students will analyze a real-world case of algorithmic bias, identify the root cause in the training data or design, and propose a concrete fix.

**Materials:** Two short, age-appropriate case study handouts (e.g., a simplified account of biased facial-recognition accuracy across skin tones; a simplified account of a resume-screening tool trained mostly on past hires), sticky notes, whiteboard.

**Warm-up (10 min):** Recall activity — "Remember the classifier you built in Week 3. What if you'd only trained it on YOUR voice/face? Who might it not work well for?"

**Direct instruction (20 min):** Present both case studies. Instructor guides discussion on the difference between a "bug" (something broken in code) and "bias" (something skewed in data or design that still runs "correctly" but unfairly). Introduce vocabulary: training data, representation, fairness.

**Small-group activity (30 min):** Groups of 3–4 take one case study, identify (a) what data was used to train the system, (b) who it likely underperformed for, and (c) one concrete fix (more diverse data, human review step, testing before release). Groups present their fix to the class.

**Wrap-up & reflection (10 min):** "Think About It" discussion: Is it ever OK to use an AI tool you know has a bias problem? Bridge to Week 8: "Next week we start writing real code — and you'll get to inspect a model's outputs yourself."

**Differentiation:** Provide a simplified one-paragraph version of each case for students who need it; extension groups research a second real case independently.

**Checks for understanding:** Each group must state the fix in one sentence before moving on; instructor collects sticky-note exit tickets: "One thing that surprised me about bias in AI today."

### Assessment Approach

Rubric-based on four criteria: technical execution, creativity, conceptual understanding, and ethics reflection (each scored 1–4). Peer feedback recorded during Week 10 showcase using structured feedback forms. Certificates awarded at completion; strong capstones flagged for optional inclusion in Tier 3 portfolio.

## 5. Tier 3 — AI Innovators (Ages 15–18)

### Overview

Big-idea focus (AI4K12 grade band 9–12): full depth across all five big ideas, plus generative AI/LLMs, prompt engineering, model evaluation, and societal/policy impact. This tier produces a portfolio-ready capstone suitable for college applications, competitions, or internship applications.

**Tools:** Python via Jupyter/Google Colab; pandas and scikit-learn; a public dataset source (e.g., Kaggle); a large language model API used under a supervised, school-managed account with an explicit responsible-use policy (see Section 6); TensorFlow/Keras for a guided neural-network build.

**Learning outcomes by end of tier:**
- Can clean and explore a real dataset using pandas.
- Can train, evaluate, and explain a supervised learning model (classification and regression) using scikit-learn.
- Understands at a conceptual level how neural networks and transformers/LLMs work, including tokens and training.
- Can write effective prompts and critically evaluate LLM outputs for hallucination and bias.
- Can articulate a position on an AI policy/ethics question, citing real-world evidence.
- Completes a portfolio-ready capstone project with a public presentation.

### Weekly Session Breakdown

| Wk | Topic & Objective | Key Activity | Materials |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Foundations & landscape | History of AI + current landscape (LLMs, generative AI); set up Python environment | Laptops, Colab |
| 2 | Data wrangling | pandas basics; explore and clean a real dataset | Laptops, sample dataset |
| 3 | Supervised learning I | Classification hands-on (decision tree/logistic regression) in scikit-learn | Laptops, Colab |
| 4 | Supervised learning II | Regression + evaluation metrics (accuracy, precision/recall) | Laptops, Colab |
| 5 | Neural networks & deep learning | Conceptual architecture; build a simple neural net (Keras) | Laptops, Colab |
| 6 | Computer vision | CNN concepts; build an image classifier project | Laptops, Colab |
| 7 | NLP & how LLMs work | Transformers conceptually (tokens, training); hands-on with an LLM API | Laptops, supervised API access |
| 8 | Prompt engineering & responsible use | Crafting effective prompts; hallucinations/limitations; academic integrity policy | Laptops, supervised API access |
| 9 | AI ethics, bias & society | Algorithmic bias deep dive; AI & jobs; policy overview (e.g., EU AI Act); structured debate | Debate prompts, articles |
| 10 | Capstone kickoff | Proposal writing; dataset/tool selection; project plan | Proposal template |
| 11 | Capstone build session | Instructor-supported build time; check-ins | Laptops, project tools |
| 12 | Capstone presentations & wrap-up | Present to peers/parents; portfolio packaging; next-step pathways | Presentation setup, certificates |

### Sample Full Lesson Plan — Week 8: "Prompt Engineering & Responsible Use"

**Objective:** Students will apply prompt-engineering techniques to improve LLM output quality, identify at least one hallucination in a model's response, and articulate the school's responsible-use policy in their own words.

**Materials:** Laptops with supervised, school-managed LLM API access; a "prompt lab" worksheet; printed responsible-use policy one-pager.

**Warm-up (10 min):** Students submit the same vague prompt (e.g., "tell me about history") to the LLM and compare wildly different, unfocused outputs. Discuss: what made this prompt weak?

**Direct instruction (20 min):** Instructor introduces prompt-engineering techniques: being specific, providing context/role, giving examples (few-shot), asking for a specific format, and iterating. Cover why models "hallucinate" (predicting plausible-sounding text, not looking up facts) and why fact-checking output matters.

**Hands-on lab (35 min):** Students rewrite their warm-up prompt three times, each with one added technique, and record how the output changes on their worksheet. Then, students deliberately try to get the model to state a plausible-sounding but false fact, and document exactly what happened.

**Wrap-up & reflection (15 min):** Review the school's responsible-use / academic-integrity policy for AI tools. Discussion: "Where's the line between using AI to help you learn and using it to avoid learning?" Bridge to Week 9's ethics/society unit.

**Differentiation:** Provide a technique cheat-sheet for students who want a scaffold; extension students attempt a multi-step reasoning prompt and evaluate whether the model's reasoning is sound.

**Checks for understanding:** Worksheet collected as a formative check; each student states one hallucination they found and one technique that most improved their output.

### Capstone Project

Individually or in pairs, students choose a real problem and build an end-to-end AI solution: a trained classifier or regression model on a public dataset, a computer vision app, or an LLM-powered tool built with responsible-use guardrails. Deliverables: working code/notebook, a one-page project write-up, and a 5-minute public presentation. Strong capstones are packaged into a portfolio suitable for college applications, science fairs, or competitions (e.g., AI4ALL-style programs).

### Assessment Approach

Rubric across four dimensions: technical depth, originality, ethical reasoning, and presentation quality (each 1–5). Written reflection required alongside the capstone. Portfolio packet issued to students on request for college/competition use.

## 6. Cross-Cutting Ethics & Safety Framework

Ethics is woven into every session (see the "Think About It" close in each lesson plan above), but the emphasis shifts by tier:

| Theme | Tier 1 (8–10) | Tier 2 (11–14) | Tier 3 (15–18) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Privacy | Don't share personal info with AI tools; always ask a trusted adult | Understand what data an app collects and why | Evaluate a tool's data/privacy policy before using it |
| Bias & fairness | "Unfair training data" intuition via games | Real-world case studies + propose fixes | Policy-level analysis (regulation, accountability) |
| Misinformation/deepfakes | Not covered directly | Intro: "AI can create fake images/text" | Deep dive: detecting synthetic media, source-checking |
| Healthy use / over-reliance | AI is a tool, not a friend or authority | When to trust vs. double-check an AI answer | Academic integrity policy; avoiding over-reliance on LLMs |
| Safe tool access | All accounts/logins managed by instructor, no minor sign-ups on adult-only tools | School-managed accounts for any tool requiring sign-up | Supervised, school-managed LLM API access with logging and an explicit responsible-use agreement signed by student & parent |

**Operating rule for the school:** any AI tool used in class that has a minimum age requirement in its terms of service (many generative AI chat tools require 13+ or 18+, or parental consent under 13) must be accessed only through an instructor- or school-managed account, never an individual minor account, consistent with COPPA-conscious practice.

## 7. Assessment & Certification

- **Tier 1 — AI Explorer:** completion certificate + digital badge; no scored assessment.
- **Tier 2 — AI Builder:** completion certificate + capstone rubric score (technical execution, creativity, understanding, ethics reflection).
- **Tier 3 — AI Innovator:** completion certificate + capstone rubric score (technical depth, originality, ethical reasoning, presentation) + optional portfolio packet for college/competition use.

A learner completing all three tiers earns a cumulative "Codeillustrated AI Pathway" certificate, positioned on the academy website as a multi-year track rather than three unrelated courses.

---

## 8. Instructor & Logistics Notes

- **Cadence:** one live session per week per cohort; tiers can run back-to-back or in parallel across age groups.
- **Class size:** kept small (10–20 max, scaling up by age) to preserve hands-on instructor support during build time.
- **Tech requirements:** one laptop/tablet with a webcam and browser per student (or pair, in Tier 1); reliable internet for cloud tools (Teachable Machine, Colab, ML4Kids); no local software installs required until optional Tier 3 extensions.
- **Parent communication:** brief end-of-tier note home summarizing what was covered and showcasing the capstone/final project; responsible-use agreement sent home for signature before Tier 3's LLM API module.
- **Staffing:** Tier 1 benefits from a co-facilitator/aide given the younger age group; Tiers 2–3 can run with one instructor plus optional TA support during capstone weeks.
