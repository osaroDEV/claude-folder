Div, Span & Semantic Layout

Today you'll learn how professional developers group and organize content — including a set of tags that describe the actual meaning of each section.

🎯 Learning Objectives
  • Use <div> to group block-level content
  • Use <span> to wrap small pieces of inline content
  • Explain the difference between block-level and inline elements
  • Use semantic HTML5 elements to structure a full page

1<div>: The Generic Block Container

A <div> (division) is a generic box with no meaning of its own — it exists purely to group other elements together, usually so they can be styled or positioned as one unit later with CSS. It's block-level, meaning it always starts on a new line and takes up the full width available.

div-example.html
<div>
  <h2>Contact Card</h2>
  <p>Email: student@example.com</p>
</div>

2<span>: The Generic Inline Container

A <span> does the same grouping job as a div, but it's inline — it doesn't force a new line, and only wraps as much space as its content needs. Use it to target a small piece of text inside a sentence.

span-example.html
<p>Your order total is <span>$24.99</span>.</p>

By itself, a <span> changes nothing visually — its power comes later when you attach CSS classes to style just that word or phrase differently from the rest of the sentence.

3Semantic HTML5 Elements

Div and span are useful, but they say nothing about what content actually is. HTML5 introduced semantic elements — tags that describe their purpose, so both browsers and developers instantly understand a page's structure.

ElementRepresents
<header>Introductory content — usually a logo, title, or intro area
<nav>A block of navigation links
<main>The primary, unique content of the page (only one per page)
<section>A distinct, themed group of content, often with its own heading
<article>Self-contained content that could stand alone, like a blog post
<aside>Content related to, but separate from, the main content (like a sidebar)
<footer>Closing content — copyright, contact info, links

4Structuring a Full Page

Here's how these elements typically combine into a real page layout:

layout.html
<body>
  <header>
    <h1>My Website</h1>
    <nav>...links...</nav>
  </header>

  <main>
    <section>
      <h2>About Me</h2>
      <p>...</p>
    </section>
  </main>

  <footer>
    <p>&copy; 2026</p>
  </footer>
</body>
💡 Rule of Thumb

Reach for a semantic tag first — it exists for almost every common section of a page. Only fall back to a plain <div> or <span> when nothing semantic truly fits.

📚 Key Vocabulary
Block-level
Starts on a new line and fills the available width
Inline
Flows within a line of text, taking only needed space
Semantic element
A tag that describes the meaning of its content
div / span
Generic block/inline containers with no inherent meaning
🚀 Challenges

Three challenges, each a little tougher than the last. Challenge 1 is the must-do; Challenges 2 and 3 are where you level up.

Challenge 1CoreRebuild Your About Me Page

Take your about-me.html page and rebuild its structure using <header>, <main>, at least two <section> elements, and a <footer>. Use one <span> somewhere to wrap a single important word or number.

Challenge 2Level UpThe Block vs. Inline Experiment

Create experiment.html. Inside the <body>, write three <div>s with one word each (Red, Green, Blue), then three <span>s with one word each (Cat, Dog, Fish). Open it in your browser.

experiment.html
<div>Red</div>
<div>Green</div>
<div>Blue</div>

<span>Cat</span>
<span>Dog</span>
<span>Fish</span>

What happened to each group? Write your answer as an HTML comment at the bottom of the file. Then add a table with two columns — Block-level and Inline — and sort these tags into it: <p>, <h2>, <a>, <strong>, <em>, <ul>, <section>, <span>. Not sure about one? Test it in your experiment file!

Challenge 3Boss LevelSchool Newspaper Front Page

Build news.html — the front page of your own school newspaper (give it a name, like The Classroom Times). It must have:

  • A <header> with an <h1> and a <nav> of three links (use href="#" for now)
  • A <main> containing two <article>s — each with its own <h2>, a short paragraph, and the date wrapped in a <span>
  • An <aside> with a "Did You Know?" fun fact
  • A <footer> with &copy; and the year

The rule: no plain <div>s allowed. If you really think you need one, write a comment explaining why no semantic tag fits.

✅ Recap

<div> and <span> are generic containers (block and inline) used for grouping. Semantic elements like header, nav, main, section, article, aside, and footer describe actual meaning and should be preferred whenever they fit. Next lesson: embedding audio, video, and other content.
